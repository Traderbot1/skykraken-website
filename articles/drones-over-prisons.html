Drones over prisons: detecting contraband drops

The drone has become the smuggler's tool of choice — flying drugs, phones and weapons over the wall in seconds. For correctional facilities, detection is no longer optional.

In short

Drone smuggling into prisons is surging: England and Wales recorded 1,712 drone incidents between April 2024 and March 2025, up 43% on the previous year. Drones deliver drugs, phones and weapons over perimeter walls, often run by organized crime. Because most facilities cannot legally bring a drone down, the priority is to detect the delivery, locate the operator, and document the incident. Portable, passive RF detection does all three — identifying the drone, mapping its path, and pinpointing the person on the ground — without infrastructure or transmitting anything.

A few years ago, contraband entered a prison hidden in mail, in visits, or through a corrupt insider. Today it can arrive by air. A small drone lifts off from a field or a car park near the perimeter, climbs over the wall, releases a package into a yard or to a cell window, and is gone in under a minute. The economics are brutal: a drone costing around a thousand euros can deliver a payload worth many times that, again and again.

This is not a fringe problem. It has become one of the defining security challenges for correctional facilities — and the numbers show it is accelerating.

The scale of the problem

In England and Wales, prisons recorded 1,712 drone incidents between April 2024 and March 2025 — a 43% increase on the previous twelve months. Globally, drones have become a preferred tool for contraband drops at correctional facilities, with law-enforcement operations repeatedly intercepting deliveries of drugs, phones and other prohibited items.

The contents matter beyond the obvious. Mobile phones let incarcerated individuals continue to run criminal operations from inside. Drugs fuel debt, violence and coercion within the facility. As one analysis put it, these incursions erode institutional discipline and feed the gang networks operating both inside and outside the walls — placing staff, inmates and the wider public at heightened risk.

Why prisons are hard to protect — and what actually helps

Prisons present a specific challenge. They are often in or near urban areas, with a noisy radio and visual environment. Walls stop people, not aircraft. And the staff on duty are corrections officers, not radio engineers — any tool has to be usable without specialist training.

Three properties make passive RF detection well suited to the corrections environment:

It locates the operator, not just the package

A contraband drone is almost always flown from nearby by an accomplice — within a few hundred metres, often in direct line of sight of the wall. Recovering a dropped package addresses the symptom; locating the operator addresses the cause. RF detection reveals the pilot's position, letting staff or police intervene against the person organizing the delivery.

It is passive and needs no infrastructure

A passive system transmits nothing — important in a sensitive, regulated environment — and a portable one requires no construction, no permanent towers, no rewiring of a secure site. It can be deployed quickly and even repositioned as patterns of activity shift.

It documents the incident

Recurring drone deliveries are also a pattern that can be built into a case. A detection that produces an identified aircraft, a mapped trajectory, the operator's location and a timestamped, integrity-sealed record turns each incursion from an isolated nuisance into evidence — the kind that supports prosecution of an organized smuggling operation rather than a single recovered package.

An honest note: the most determined operators may use very small or pre-programmed drones that are harder to detect, which is why no single method is a complete answer and serious facilities layer their defences. But the large majority of prison-delivery drones are consumer models that communicate over radio or broadcast Remote ID — exactly what passive RF detection is built to catch.

From detection to deterrence

There is a second-order effect worth naming. When a facility can reliably detect deliveries, locate operators and build evidence, the risk calculation for the smuggler changes. A delivery route that once felt anonymous and consequence-free becomes one where the person on the ground can be located and identified. Detection is not only a response tool; over time it is a deterrent.
